The short version

Three things to land in the room

1. The growth was planned and is finished. 21 → 34 → 47 is two steps of exactly +13. A Talos cluster is 13 VMs. We built QA in July and Production at the end of July, so that changes could stop being tested in production. That programme is complete — there is no third build queued.

2. Eleven VMs are unexplained by our clusters. Terraform state says we manage exactly 36 of the 47 — 282 vCPU and 660 GB of the 314 and 704 Jon counts. The other 11 predate the June count and are not in the current Terraform. Finding and removing them is the first thing I will do with Jon's spreadsheet.

3. Talos is 3% of the storage on that cluster. The vCenter holds 383 VMs and 366 TB provisioned. Talos is 10.98 TB of it. Four database VMs alone hold 88 TB — eight times the entire Talos estate. Storage is worth discussing; it is not a Talos conversation.

The application pool is the whole story. Five nodes at 16 vCPU, 32 GB and 800 GB each make up 65% of the vCPU, 59% of the RAM and 78% of the disk of a cluster. Everything else — control plane, system, platform — is rounding by comparison.

The forecast risk

Reserved 10.79 TB, using 2.7 TB

This is the number I would watch, not CPU. A Kubernetes cluster reserves its disk up front and fills it as logs, container images, database volumes and the Ceph storage layer accumulate. Nothing has to change for 2.7 TB to become 5, then 8.

The application pool's Ceph disks — 500 GB per node, 2.5 TB per cluster — are the largest single contributor and the one most likely to fill quietly.

How the footprint grows from here

The unit of growth is a cluster, not a VM

Talos clusters do not grow one machine at a time. They come in fixed shapes, and that makes forecasting unusually clean:

Saying it without the jargon

Lines you can use in the room

Why three of everything

We build the same platform three times on purpose — a workbench, a dress rehearsal, and the live stage. Before July we had only the workbench and the live stage, which meant changes were rehearsed in production. QA is what stopped that.

Why a cluster is not 13 servers

A cluster is 13 machines that only function as a set. You cannot power three of them off on a Friday to free up room, any more than you can remove three cylinders from an engine. The unit we add or remove is the whole cluster.

Why the disk number will keep climbing

We reserved nearly eleven terabytes and have filled under three. The reservation is a bill we already agreed to; the usage is what we pay today. That gap closes on its own.

Where the capacity actually goes

Two thirds of every cluster is one pool of five machines that the applications themselves run on. The plumbing is cheap. The tenants are what cost.

What we are asking for

Not more capacity. We want to give some back — starting with eleven machines nobody has claimed, and a test environment that is sized like production without carrying production's load.

Method

How we tell ours from everything else

So the list we bring back is defensible, and nothing gets powered off on an assumption.

A VM is ours if Terraform is tracking it. That is the record that decides what gets rebuilt, resized or destroyed by the pipeline, so it is the only definition that means anything operationally. We read it from the three cluster state files — one per AWS account, since each environment keeps its own — and cross-check against the machines actually registered in each live cluster.

Two independent sources matter here. A VM present in a cluster but absent from Terraform is drift, not an orphan: something we depend on that the pipeline has lost track of. Deleting one of those would take a working cluster down. They come out of the report in their own bucket, flagged as do-not-touch.

Everything in Jon's inventory that appears in neither is a candidate, and the word is doing work. "Not in our Terraform" is not the same as "nobody's" — it needs an owner to confirm before anything is powered off.

The failure this is built to avoid

If one state file cannot be read — an expired SSO session, a wrong profile, a bucket in the wrong account — a naive script reports that cluster's thirteen VMs as unmanaged. They look exactly like orphans. Acting on that would destroy a production cluster.

So the tool refuses to classify anything unless every source loads: three credentialed sessions verified against their expected account numbers, three state files parsed, and a non-empty VM list from each. An incomplete picture exits with an error instead of a tidy-looking answer. It also prints which spreadsheet column it read names from, because a silently mismatched column classifies the entire fleet as "not ours".

We hit precisely this class of error twice in the last week — a probe reporting five production secrets as missing minutes after they were created, because the region was not pinned. The stakes here are higher, so the guard is louder.

Running it

python3 scripts/talos-vm-reconcile.py prints what we manage, with the vCPU, memory and disk each VM reserves. --diff <inventory.csv> classifies Jon's export into ours, not ours, and in Terraform but not in the inventory — that last bucket catching VMs the pipeline thinks it owns that no longer exist. The script only reads; it emits no destroy commands, by design.
